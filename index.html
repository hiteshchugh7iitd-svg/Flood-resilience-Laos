The study: question, objectives, hypothesis

A qualitative comparative multiple-case study of four flood-exposed government schools in one district, taking the school-level decision episode as the unit of analysis. The questions below are those of the consolidated proposal; the fourth was added after the LESMIS audit reported on this platform.

Main research question

How did principals in four flood-exposed government schools receive, interpret and use flood-warning information when deciding whether and when to suspend on-site classes during a recent flood event?

RQ1 · Warning communication

Through which source and channel, at what time, and with what content did flood-warning information reach each principal — and does the office named as the source match the legal warning-issuing authority under Article 18 of the Law on Disaster Management?

RQ2 · Principal decision-making

How did message actionability, credibility, confirmation-seeking, local environmental cues and decision authority shape the timing of suspension — and how did decision latency compare with the effective lead time available?

RQ3 · Immediate school response

What arrangements followed for communication with teachers and families, safe student dismissal, reopening, and recovery of missed instructional time?

RQ4 · The institutional record — added after the audit

What record of the closure decision was created at school, district and ministry level; where does it reside; and what does its presence or absence indicate about accountability for the decision?

Research aim

To explain how flood-warning information was translated into decisions on whether and when to suspend on-site classes; to identify the communication, decision and organisational conditions that supported or constrained timely action and educational continuity; and to establish what institutional record, if any, the decision left behind.

Objectives

  1. Reconstruct the source, channel, timing and content of the warning information each principal received.
  2. Examine how principals interpreted and verified it, and how authority and local conditions affected decision timing.
  3. Compare decision latency against the effective lead time available in each case.
  4. Compare two primary and two lower-secondary schools for common mechanisms and meaningful differences.
  5. Document immediate safety, communication, reopening and learning-recovery arrangements.
  6. Establish what record exists at each administrative level, and confirm with the Education and Sports Statistics Centre and the National Disaster Management Office whether their systems capture it.
  7. Establish whether school-level users were consulted when the current procedures were designed, and relate that to the gaps observed.
  8. Develop feasible recommendations on message actionability, acknowledgement, decision authority, continuity planning and the recording of closures.

Hypothesis

The binding constraint on protective action in Lao schools is institutional, not meteorological: warning information does reach schools, but through channels nobody owns, to a decision-maker holding no documented mandate, leaving no record — so further investment in detection alone will not change what happens at the school gate.

The proposition is tested by matching each case, after coding, to one of four patterns fixed before fieldwork. Because the patterns are specified in advance, it is their distribution across the cases — not a reading of any single case — that indicates where the constraint lies. A case fitting no pattern is reported as such.

A · Institutional gap
Usable information received and understood, but mandate, role, coordination or capacity absent; action delayed or inconsistent. The constraint is institutional readiness.
B · Communication gap
Information not received inside the effective lead time, or received but not understood as locally relevant and actionable. The constraint lies upstream, in content, channel or relay.
C · Working chain
Received, understood, all four elements present, action timely and communicated. A disconfirming case, examined closely for what made it work.
D · Contextual constraint
Received, institutional elements present, but timing governed by external conditions or by lead time already exhausted. Delay is not evidence of institutional failure.

Provisional against the twelve Luang Namtha responses collected in September 2026: Schools A and C sort to A, and School D — the only school leader telephoned by the district office, who confirmed receipt and acted inside a five-hour lead time — sits closest to C while still leaving no entry in the national record. What this platform establishes

Flood early-warning systems deliver protection only when information reaches an authorised decision-maker in time, is understood as locally relevant, and can be acted upon within the remaining lead time. For a school, that means a named person deciding whether to continue classes, dismiss early, delay opening, or suspend teaching on site.

Two findings, both established from documentary evidence before any school visit, define the problem the education sector now faces.

First, the decision is not recorded. A systematic audit of the Lao Education and Sports Management Information System confirms that the national data architecture records whether a school exists, not whether it operated on a given day. The status register captures permanent institutional dissolution only. The building register records physical condition with no field for cause and no event date. Across all 676 flagged building records nationally, flooding is named in two free-text notes.

Second, the decision is not assigned. Article 18 of the Law on Disaster Management (No. 71/NA, 2019) places the authority to issue a flood warning with the territorial administrative head, not with the meteorological service. A full-text search of that Law, the national early-warning standard operating procedure, and the current inter-agency contingency plan returns no provision naming the school as a recipient of warning information at any layer.

Taken together: the sector cannot currently demonstrate how many schools closed in any flood season, for how long, on whose authority, or how many children lost instructional time. That is a measurement gap with a direct operational cost, and it is addressable.

Alignment with international guidance

The World Bank's Choosing Our Future: Education for Climate Action (2024) reports that more than 400 million students have experienced weather-related school closures since 2022, and that students in low-income countries lose an average of 18 school days a year to climate disruption against 2.4 days in wealthier countries. Among its recommendations to education ministries is the systematic tracking of closures.

Establishing a closure record is therefore not an additional reporting burden. It is the measurement step that international guidance already identifies as a precondition for managing climate disruption to learning.

Three proposals for the sector
  1. A closure field on the school record. Date, duration, hazard cause and deciding authority — four values, entered once per event, joined on the existing School ID.
  2. A named warning recipient at each school. The four-element check applied in this platform tests mandate, role, coordination and capacity; all four are currently undocumented at school level.
  3. An acknowledgement step in the warning chain. The Early Warnings for All Roadmap identifies the absence of any receipt-verification mechanism as a national gap; the education chain is where it is most easily closed.

Theory and framework

A flood warning system is not a technology but a sequence of institutional transfers between actors at different levels of authority. Research on early warning finds that such systems fail at the transfers — the links — rather than at the point of forecast. For schools, which sit inside an administrative hierarchy with rules about who may close and what must be reported, the final transfers have not been examined. The study traces one warning through six of them, using three bodies of theory that together make a prediction the field data can test.

Conclusion

The vulnerability is not in the components of the warning system but in the institutional links between them. The remedy is not to eliminate informal institutions — which are adaptive and fast — but to formalise the gaps they fill: a named sender with required acknowledgement, pre-delegated authority on defined triggers, and a closure field in the education data system.

The record gap

A systematic review of the Lao Education and Sports Management Information System was conducted on 28 August 2026 through the public LESMIS Hub operated by the Education and Sports Statistics Centre. Its purpose was to establish whether a temporary school closure is recorded anywhere in the national education data architecture.

Principal finding

LESMIS records whether a school exists. It does not record whether that school operated on a given day.

The School Status report uses ຍຸບ (yup) — dissolved, abolished, merged out of existence. That is permanent institutional closure. The Student Life Cycle module tracks seven items, all properties of the student. A flood closure surfaces only as absenteeism, indistinguishable from illness.

Mandate: who may warn, and who must act

Institutional mapping identifies which organisations are active; it does not establish what the law requires any of them to do. On 2 September 2026 the governing instruments were read in primary text — the Law on Disaster Management, its implementing decrees, the national early-warning standard operating procedure and the current inter-agency contingency plan — rather than in secondary summary.

Principal finding

The word “school” does not appear in the Law on Disaster Management. Across the statute, the operational procedure and the current contingency plan, the school appears only as a site of damage to be repaired — never as a recipient of warning information with a decision to make.

This is a negative finding from a deliberate full-text search across three legal and operational layers, not an inference from the absence of contrary evidence.

The warning is not, in law, the meteorological service’s to issue

Article 18 of the Law on Disaster Management (No. 71/NA, 24 July 2019) assigns the legal act of issuing a disaster warning to the territorial administrative head. The Department of Meteorology and Hydrology supplies the forecast, monitoring and estimation on which that warning rests: it forecasts; it does not, in law, warn. Article 19 assigns the same territorial authority responsibility for announcing evacuation.

Why this matters operationally

For a flood confined to one district — Luang Namtha in August 2026, or any other — the legal warning issuer is the Head of District — the same administrative office to which the District Education and Sports Bureau reports. The warning and the school-closure instruction originate, in law, from a single office. Where principals nonetheless report receiving nothing usable, the diagnosis is not a slow relay: the authority that issued the warning and the authority over the schools were the same body, and the message still did not arrive.

Public reporting through 2026 nonetheless describes the meteorological service itself as issuing flood warnings. There is therefore a documented and testable divergence between the legal allocation of the warning function and its exercise in practice.

The diagnostic question

“When a flood warning reaches this district, whose name or signature is on it — the weather department, the district administration, or someone else?”

Put identically to the National Disaster Management Office, the District Education and Sports Bureau, and each principal. Divergent answers locate the break precisely.

Verification note

The Law was read in the unofficial English translation published by the International Federation of Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies. The articles are plain and the committee lists unambiguous, so mistranslation risk is low — but because Article 18 is load-bearing, its translation should be confirmed against the Lao original before formal citation.

Education’s seat: present at every tier, directive at none

Article 51 describes the Disaster Management Committee at every tier as a non-standing body with an advisory role. Article 52 provides that any ministry, where necessary, may establish a ministry-level committee — a permissive clause with no minimum content, specifying neither a meeting schedule nor a function.

Where the chains fail to meet

At district level the District Disaster Management Committee and the District Education and Sports Bureau both report to the same Head of District — the office holding legal warning power under Article 18. No instrument specifies a transfer between them. In its place, principals report an informal messaging group as the working channel. Whether any office above them treats that channel as official is the single most consequential open question in the chain.

Geographic method

District boundaries are geoBoundaries gbOpen ADM2 (CC BY 4.0), derived from National Geographic Department and OCHA Regional Office for Asia and the Pacific sources. All 148 polygons are joined to their administrative record by an explicit alias table, with duplicate district names resolved by centroid position and every join verified by point-in-polygon test against province boundaries.

River geometry is Natural Earth 10m centrelines, clipped to the national bounding box. Mekong proximity is computed as great-circle distance from each school point to the nearest mainstem vertex.

The contradiction the three instruments expose

Asked who holds the final authority to close a school for flooding, both district education officers named their own office. Asked the same question, three of four school leaders said they decided alone and needed no permission — and both district offices confirmed that no principal has ever closed a school without telling them first. Nobody is disobeying anyone. The authority is simply asserted at two levels at once, and no respondent at either level could produce a document that settles it: one office named a provincial instruction without a reference, the other left the question blank.

This is what an undocumented handoff looks like from the inside, and it is the single clearest case for writing the procedure down.

Heatwave early warning and school response in Delhi

Why heat remains the least institutionalised school hazard

An empirical study of institutional gaps in West Delhi government schools, through the School Heatwave Early Warning Governance Framework (SHEW-GF)

Abstract

Background
Delhi issues accurate, public heatwave warnings, and its government schools still open their gates to children on days above 42°C without a reliable, coordinated protective response. On twenty-five days in 2025 the city reached or exceeded 42°C; no record exists of what its schools did on those days. The same schools respond to a fire alarm immediately and uniformly, and the same city governs air pollution — another slow-onset hazard — through a graded plan tied to stated thresholds. Heat is therefore not a failure of warning but a failure of conversion: the institutional distance between a warning that exists and a child who is protected.
Theory
Warning-response research explains conversion well for an individual deciding for themselves, most completely in the Protective Action Decision Model (PADM; Lindell & Perry, 2012). A school breaks three of that model's assumptions at once: the person at risk (the child) is not the decision-maker; the decision-maker (the principal) may not be permitted to decide; and the decision distributes protection unequally among people who did not decide. Repairing these assumptions with the Mental Models Approach, institutional theory, street-level bureaucracy, policy implementation and instrument theory, risk-perception research, the Access model of vulnerability and organisational learning produces the study's framework, SHEW-GF: a six-link chain — Anticipation, Reception, Interpretation, Authorisation, Execution and Retention — read along two axes, institutionalisation depth and protective distribution, and governed by a claim of sequential nullification, under which a failure at any link cancels every capacity downstream of it.
Design
A qualitative, explanatory, design-oriented multiple-case study of ten government schools in Directorate of Education Zones West-A and West-B, with an embedded cross-hazard comparison (fire, earthquake, air pollution) that holds the institution constant while varying the hazard. Heat episodes from the 2025 and 2026 seasons are reconstructed through episode-centred interviews with principals, teachers and parents, decision vignettes with a fire-alarm control, infrastructure and records observation, documentary analysis, and interviews with officials of the Directorate of Education, DDMA, the health sector, implementers and experts. Analysis uses the Framework Method, theory-testing process tracing and within-school cross-hazard comparison.
Expected findings and outputs
The central proposition is that heat fails earlier in the chain than fire: Delhi's heat instruments are almost entirely awareness instruments issued into a chain broken at anticipation, reception, authorisation and retention. The endpoint is design: a threshold-based standard operating procedure, a decision matrix, a communication protocol, a readiness checklist and a closure record, each generated by the link found to fail and each tested against the child who cannot be collected. The study arrives as heatwave has been added to India's notified disaster list (August 2026), so that the funds now available have something specific to pay for.
“When the system writes about fire it reaches for a number. When it writes about heat it improvises.”
The same school system converts other warnings competently. A fire alarm in a Delhi government school produces an immediate, rehearsed, uniform response, because fire has been specified: extinguishers are installed to Fire Department specifications, drills are held and recorded, and someone is answerable. Within a single DoE circular, fire safety is written against an external standard while heat is written in adjectives — "adequate" drinking water, cooling systems in "proper working order".

Problem statement

This is not a failure of warning. The alert is issued, it is accurate, and it is public. It is a failure of conversion — the distance between a warning that exists and a child who is cooler. That distance is the subject of this thesis.

That asymmetry cannot be explained by speed of onset, because air pollution defeats that explanation. Air quality in Delhi is slow-onset like heat, and it is governed by GRAP, which ties action to stated AQI bands and names which classes stop at which threshold. One slow-onset environmental hazard in this school system is already institutionalised. Whatever explains heat's position must therefore be specific to heat — and, this thesis argues, specific to how heat has been instructed.

The research gap

Theoretical gap
Warning-response research has a mature model of how an individual converts a warning into protection, and no equivalent model for an institution converting a warning into protection on someone else's behalf. The three assumptions PADM makes — that the person at risk decides, that they are free to decide, and that the action protects the decider — all fail in a school, and they fail in schools in every country, not only in Delhi. There is no framework that holds the sequence together once those assumptions are removed.
Empirical gap
No study has traced a heatwave alert from IMD through DDMA and the Directorate of Education into a Delhi government school and out again as an action or a non-action. The chain has been assumed by every document that refers to it and observed by nobody. What schools actually did during the twenty-five days of 2025 is not recorded anywhere, which is itself one of the findings this study expects to make. Nor has any study compared, within the same schools, how heat and other hazards are institutionalised.
Practical gap
Until August 2026 the binding constraint on school heat protection could plausibly be described as money. It no longer can. With SDRF and mitigation finance open to heat measures, the constraint has moved to specification: nobody has established what a Delhi school would need written down, delegated to it or installed in it in order to act.

Why this matters now

Two developments turn this into an urgent design problem. First, with heatwave now a notified disaster, states previously capped at 10% of annual SDRF for heat relief can draw on full SDRF and mitigation-fund allocations. Money has arrived for procedures nobody has written. Second, Delhi's own 2025 Heat Action Plan omitted schools. If the binding constraint on school heat protection was once money, it is now specification: what a school would need written down, delegated to it, channelled to it or installed in it in order to act. A recommendation that schools "should be prepared" reproduces the gap in the imperative mood.

SHEW-GF: the School Heatwave Early Warning Governance Framework

The School Heatwave Early Warning Governance Framework (SHEW-GF) is an institutional model of warning conversion. It takes the sequence that warning-response research established for individuals and rebuilds it for the case in which an organisation must convert a warning into protection for people who are not themselves deciding. It has four components: a chain of six links through which a warning must pass to become protection; a governing claim of sequential nullification; Axis I, which measures how deeply each link is institutionalised; and Axis II, which asks for whom each link's protection works. It is used in this study in three ways: as a diagnostic tool (where did the chain break in each school?), as a comparative tool (how does heat's profile differ from fire's in the same building?) and as a design tool (which instrument would repair the link found to fail?).

Sequential nullification, worked

The framework's governing claim is sequential nullification: a failure at any link cancels every capacity downstream of it. A school that anticipates, receives, understands and is authorised, but has no working tap, protects nobody. A school with excellent taps that never received the alert protects nobody either. Following Pressman and Wildavsky's logic of clearance points, if p₁ to p₆ are the probabilities that each link holds in a given episode, the probability that a warning converts into protection is their product. P(conversion) = p₁ × p₂ × p₃ × p₄ × p₅ × p₆. Move the sliders to set how likely each link is to hold in an episode.

Three consequences follow. First, conversion is conjunctive: each link is a necessary condition, and no link can compensate for another. Second, a chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and improving a strong link while a weak one stays broken changes almost nothing. Third, the diagnostic question for any school is not "how well is it doing?" but "where did the chain first break?" This is what makes the framework diagnostic rather than descriptive.

Axis II refines the claim. The product is not the same for every child: for a child who cannot be collected, p₅ for early closure may be close to zero even when it is high for her classmates. The framework therefore asks for conversion per group of children, not only per school.

Axis II — Protective distribution

Axis II asks the Access model's question at every link: when this link works, for whom does it work? It is asked throughout the chain rather than once at the end because distribution is shaped at every step — who receives the alert, whose risk is understood, whose interests the decision reflects, whose classroom is hottest, and whose experience is recorded.

The sharpest case sits at Execution. Early closure protects a child with a cool home and an available guardian; the same decision at the same hour exposes a child whose guardian is at work and whose home is one room under a metal roof. The instruction is identical; the protection is not. The groups the study attends to include the youngest children (Classes I–II), children on upper floors, second-shift children who arrive at the day's peak, children who walk long distances, and children with no adult at home during an early closure.

Mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery in Delhi schools, with Japan as reference. What happens between a heatwave alert being issued and a Delhi school acting on it?

Field study at a glance

Study site
Ten government schools in Directorate of Education Zones West-A and West-B, the city's hottest and most exposed area.
Method
Interviews with principals and teachers, observation in the cool and hot seasons, and a document review of each circular.
Status
Field study pending Kobe University ethics approval and Directorate of Education permission.

Abstract

Delhi schools operate under a Heat Action Plan, education-sector circulars, impact-based warnings from the India Meteorological Department (IMD) and national school safety guidelines. These instruments are largely reactive: they name actions without tying them to an alert level, a decision-maker, a lead time or a record, and they leave schools to manage heat within the limits of their staffing, coordination between departments and accountability. Existing research on heatwaves describes thermal, health, leadership and infrastructure constraints, but rarely follows a warning down through school authority, timelines and responsibilities.

This paper compares the instruments that govern school heat in Delhi with Japan’s arrangements across the four phases of disaster management: mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery. Japan’s instruments are graded, measured and recorded, and they state who decides and when. Delhi’s instruments concentrate on response and lack a dated pre-season directive, action graded by alert level, and rules for what happens to children after a hot day. It also compares school practice in the two countries on timing, cooling, risk indicators, first aid and dress.

From this comparison the paper proposes a four-phase model for school heatwave governance with twenty-one nodes, running from a dated pre-season directive to a post-event record, and a heat day counter with which a school can anticipate escalation from published IMD criteria. The policies and instructions provide the expert model; a proposed field study of principals and teachers in ten government schools in West Delhi would provide the lay model and test where practice departs from it. The model requires no new policy and works within existing central and state guidelines.

When is it a heatwave in India?

India Meteorological Department (n.d.) has defined a heatwave only above a floor of 40°C in the plains, 37°C at coastal stations or 30°C in hilly regions. Above that floor, declaration proceeds on a departure from the 1991–2020 station normal of 4.5°C to 6.4°C, severe above 6.4°C, or on an absolute maximum of 45°C, severe at 47°C. The criteria must be met at two stations in a sub-division on two consecutive days, and the declaration is made on the second day.

Wet-bulb globe temperature

Japan's arrangements use WBGT, combining air temperature, humidity, radiant heat and air movement; a gymnasium can be more dangerous than a schoolyard at the same air temperature. The Heatstroke Alert is issued at a forecast daily maximum WBGT of 33°C, the Special Alert at 35°C across a prefecture (MOE, 2024; Oka et al., 2023).

The intended alert chain

{{ c.name }}→

For the chain to be effective, four of its properties must be established rather than assumed: whether schools appear as an institutional recipient category; the medium and addressee at each transfer; the interval between declaration and school awareness; and whether receipt is confirmed at every stage.

Two design features of the chain are already known to constrain it. The first is that the declaration is made on the second consecutive day of qualifying conditions (IMD, n.d.), so any instrument conditioned on declaration inherits this lag. The second is that the 2026 circular specifies what the receiving end must do but does not clearly specify how the alert should be sent in a timely and reliable way.

Why preparedness is the structural gap

Indian heatwave governance is at present reactive: the policies address the response after a heatwave alert is issued, because preparedness is not yet institutionalized in Indian schools. Fire and earthquake are institutionalised in Indian schools principally through preparedness: a drill register, a certificate, a named focal-point teacher, a plan, none of which depends on an alarm being issued. None of the Delhi instruments reviewed in Sections 3.2 and 3.5 establishes an equivalent heat-specific pre-season phase with a named owner.

What happens to children afterwards

Early dismissal at midday can raise exposure instead of reducing it, placing children on unshaded streets at the hottest hour, so dispersal requires its own provisions: shaded waiting, supervision until collection, and an arrangement for children who walk. Two questions no Delhi instrument addresses follow. What happens to a child whose guardian cannot collect them? And may a school retain children beyond normal hours as a custodial measure, under whose authority? Japan's handbook names handover and waiting as requiring an advance policy (MOE & MEXT, 2024).

Two features govern the analysis

Two features govern the analysis. First, nodes 11 to 13 form one graded response: an instruction activates one of them and leaves the other two unmandated. Node 8, interpretation, is therefore crucial. Second, the chain moves no faster than its slowest transfer: every node downstream inherits the declaration lag described in Section 4.4.

As identified above, there is no defined public authority to receive an instruction on behalf of children, and no available literature has addressed the instruments that govern heatwave governance in India, particularly Delhi. The model tries to fill the gap. The closest prior work, the HeatReady schools approach of Shortridge et al. (2022), shares this phase logic; the present model adds the mapping to named Indian and Japanese instruments, node-level tracing of who may decide, and an escalation counter (Section 4.6).

The heat day counter

The operational question for a school is not what Orange means but whether Orange is about to become Red, because a change in alert level requires notice to guardians, a change in school timings, and notice to transport and kitchen staff, which a same-day alert does not provide. The claim is small: can a school anticipate that the situation is likely to worsen before the official alert is issued? The Indian scale makes this computable. Because the colours are defined by persistence and accumulation rather than temperature, escalation is a function of a running count a school can maintain itself, and the rules are public: Orange follows severe conditions persisting two days or non-severe conditions persisting four or more; Red follows severe conditions persisting more than two days or a seasonal total above six (IMD, n.d.).

What the counter gives a school

Three implications follow. First, a school keeping a heat day counter can see escalation approaching. The counter is a wall chart recording, each day, whether IMD declared heatwave or severe conditions for the sub-division, with a running seasonal total. On the third consecutive non-severe day, and equally on the first severe day, Orange is one day away; on the second consecutive severe day Red is one day away; and when the seasonal total reaches five Red is two days away. All it takes is a chart, a pen and the person named at node P3. Second, the notice this yields differs by limb and should not be averaged. The seasonal-total limb gives two days; the persistence limbs give one at most and, because IMD declares only on the second qualifying day, in practice part of one. Read against the IMD five-day district forecast, the combination gives a school somewhere between several hours and two days: enough to warn guardians, brief the kitchen and put the question to the zone. Third, it converts the school from passive recipient to anticipatory actor without altering any instrument.

The obvious objection is that anticipation without authority changes nothing, since a school foreseeing Red on Tuesday still cannot alter Tuesday's timings on Monday or Sunday. That understates what a dated record does, because the school can now make a formal, dated request to the authority before the likely event. Where authority is unassigned, the practical question is not who holds the power, but who carries the consequence of its not being exercised. A counter moves the school from a position where nobody asked to one, to where somebody was asked and did not answer, in writing, before the event.

Two limits

Two limits apply. The first is the input. The counter depends on the school knowing what IMD declared each day, and Section 4.4 shows the 2026 circular mandating that the daily alert be shared without granting a channel to obtain it; the proposal therefore presupposes a route, such as the IMD district bulletin or SACHET, that the instruments do not currently name. The second is the declaration lag: because IMD declares on the second consecutive day of qualifying conditions, the practical lead time is one day shorter than the raw criteria suggest. Beyond that it tracks the sub-division and not the school, and it cannot anticipate a single-day extreme. In its favour, a school could start tomorrow, at almost no cost, on public information alone.

Policy implications and the proposed field study

The proposed model tells schools what to do when a specific alert or warning is issued, and its twenty-one nodes specify all phases of the disaster: mitigation before it, preparedness ahead of the season, how management should act during the event, and recovery afterwards. The model also takes account of learning from events. It requires no new policy, as it operates within existing central and state guidelines.

Field study: ten government schools, West Delhi

The site is ten government schools in Directorate of Education Zones West-A and West-B, chosen because independent evidence marks West Delhi as the city’s hottest and most exposed area: urban heat island intensity, social vulnerability and informal settlements (Kaur & Somvanshi, 2022). This is a maximum-signal purposive sample: it can establish that a gap exists and what it looks like, not how common it is.

Six objects would be measured: what the school holds in writing, what staff understand the alert to mean, who believes they may decide what, what is physically present, what is done on a hot day, and what is recorded afterwards. The fifth requires observation against a prior account. Five instruments serve them: interviews with word-identical items put separately to principal and teachers; an observation checklist that also records the fire extinguisher servicing tag and drill register; a documentary pro forma logging each circular's operative verb, addressee, trigger and named actor; scripted vignettes; and two observation rounds, cool and hot season.

Limitations

The model is untested, and one limitation is structural as well as practical. It was derived partly from the same instruments against which Section 3 then reads Delhi and Japan, so that section demonstrates coherence and stops short of validity: a framework built to notice missing graded content will notice missing graded content. The independent test is the field study. Two elements cannot be scored from documents in any jurisdiction-what staff understand, and whether a response has become routine; and the escalation counter has not been trialled..
